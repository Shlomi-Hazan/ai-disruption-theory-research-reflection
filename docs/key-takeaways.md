# Key Takeaways

## Part 1 — The article's main results

Summary of *AI Disruption and Falling Costs in Theory Research* (Manso, Verdun & Thompson, preprint). Please see the article for exact claims and caveats.

- **Setup:** 254 questions built from recent arXiv theory papers across seven fields, answered by six OpenAI releases (o1 → GPT-5.5) and graded against rubrics derived from the human papers.
- **Accuracy:** answers matching the human result with full marks rose from 2.8% to 34.6%. Mean score rose from 29.9% to 69.1%.
- **Cost:** cost per full-mark answer fell from about $170 to about $15, driven by higher accuracy, while the cost per attempt stayed near $5.
- **Breadth:** gains appear in all seven fields, at roughly 2 score points per month.
- **Not monotonic:** some releases lose answers earlier ones solved, consistent with a "jagged frontier."
- **Caveats the authors state:** LLM grading, a small human review (five problems), one specific pipeline, model-only costs that exclude human verification, and OpenAI compute support.
- **Looking ahead:** if derivation gets cheaper faster than verification, picking good questions and establishing reliable answers may become the main constraints.

## Part 2 — Ideas from the personal reflection

These points are the reflection's own arguments and interpretation, not the article's conclusions.

## 1. The researcher's role may shift

If AI can handle a growing share of research work at low cost, the center of gravity for researchers may move from **creating knowledge independently** to **examining, validating, and critically evaluating** AI-produced results.

## 2. Verification becomes more valuable, not less

Producing outputs quickly and in large quantities is only half of the problem. Making sure they are accurate, reliable, and scientifically valuable requires knowledgeable, experienced people.

## 3. Entry-level work is the first rung of the ladder

Basic coding, testing, bug fixing, and routine maintenance are the tasks through which junior developers traditionally built experience. If AI absorbs them, the path from beginner to expert gets harder to climb.

## 4. The barrier to entry may rise

If experienced researchers face fundamental role changes, a bachelor's degree alone may stop being enough for some positions, and advanced study or research experience could become closer to a prerequisite.

## 5. Critical evaluation is a deep skill

Assessing a complex AI output means understanding the problem it tried to solve, its assumptions, its proofs and logic, and being able to challenge its conclusions. Operating the tool is not enough.

## 6. A possible enrollment problem

If the bar keeps rising while entry-level opportunities shrink, fewer young people may choose to study Computer Science and Software Engineering in the first place, at a time when the technology is advancing fastest.

## 7. The open question

If we need deep experts to challenge AI, but reduce the chances for beginners to gain experience, **how do we train the next generation of those experts?**
